# Dry Falls Cut Runoff; Columbia Untested

**Living literature review for the dry-fall-effects project. Compiled 2026-10-23 from six research-note files in `research_notes/Antecedent soil moisture snowmelt runoff/`. Update in place as citations are verified (see Appendix A and C).**

The literature supports a qualitative claim and withholds the quantitative one this project needs. Dry antecedent soil and subsurface storage lower spring runoff efficiency in snow-dominated basins. The best-documented case is the 2021 shortfall across the Intermountain West and California, when April 1 snow water equivalent (SWE) was 50-80% of normal but April-July flow was only 8-57% of normal at the same sites ([WWA WY2021 summary](https://wwa.colorado.edu/resources/intermountain-west-climate-dashboard/briefing/water-year-2021-summary)) [verified: opened]. Groundwater-memory work supports the same direction: tritium dating shows older groundwater supplies 58% +/- 34% of western snowmelt runoff ([USGS record of Brooks et al. 2025](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/groundwater-dominates-snowmelt-runoff-and-controls-streamflow-efficiency-western)) [verified: opened]. But the evidence is thin where it matters for us. Peer-reviewed forecast studies find that antecedent soil moisture adds no significant skill on average and helps mainly in anomalously dry years ([Goble and Schumacher 2023](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/64150)) [verified: opened]. The only operational number found is a Colorado-basin internal CBRFC estimate of about 0.5% lower annual runoff per 1.0% lower fall soil moisture ([CBRFC WY2021 Year in Review](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/yir/yir_2021.pdf)) [verified: opened]. The notes found **no quantified fill-and-spill threshold, no peer-reviewed regression of spring runoff efficiency on fall soil moisture, no Columbia/PNW quantification, no PNW snow-on-date trend study, and no PNW baseflow/recession study for basalt, batholith or Belt terrain.** Operational forecasters in the Columbia (NWRFC) and Colorado (CBRFC) carry soil moisture only as a simulated state in Snow-17/SAC-SMA, and CBRFC attributes roughly half of its April 1 volume error to model error, including soil-moisture state. Methodologically, the toolkit for our design exists in pieces: reverse-ESP (initial-condition swap), partial pooling, threshold tests, blocked cross-validation and reference-gauge datasets. No one has assembled it for Columbia headwaters. That is the opening for the six-basin, ~45-water-year study.

## 1. What is established, contested and unknown

**Citation convention.** Sections 2-7 give author(s), year, title, venue and DOI/URL at the first mention of each source; later mentions use short forms. The Summary below uses short forms, and Appendix B lists every cited source in full. Each claim carries an evidence tag: **[verified: opened]** means the notes record the page, abstract or PDF as fetched and read (for the snow-timing, groundwater, dataset and methods notes, a landing page or abstract recorded without a "search summary" flag; no full texts were read in those notes). **[lower confidence: search snippet only]** means the notes record only a search-result snippet or secondary summary. Researcher "Inferences" in the notes are labelled as inference throughout and are never presented as findings.

**Established (qualitatively; mostly abstract-level or agency evidence).** Several lines of evidence agree on direction.

- Dry antecedent conditions coincided with large SWE-to-runoff shortfalls in 2021 in the Upper Colorado, Great Basin, Rio Grande and California ([WWA WY2021](https://wwa.colorado.edu/resources/intermountain-west-climate-dashboard/briefing/water-year-2021-summary)) [verified: opened]. *Correction applied 2026-10-23:* an earlier draft also cited McEvoy and Hatchett (2023) here. Its Crossref abstract ("Spring heat waves drive record western United States snow melt in 2021", Environ. Res. Lett. 18, 014007) attributes the April 2021 melt to two spring heat waves and record solar radiation and does not state a dry-soil runoff-efficiency effect; it is evidence for a competing April-weather explanation, not for the dry-soil hypothesis. Whether its full text discusses antecedent soil moisture was not checked.
- Subsurface storage, not only the snowpack, controls how much melt reaches streams, and the effect persists for years ([Brooks et al. 2025](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/groundwater-dominates-snowmelt-runoff-and-controls-streamflow-efficiency-western); [Hammond, Kampf et al. 2024](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70258230)) [verified: opened].
- Geology governs recession and the sensitivity of summer flow to snow loss in the Oregon Cascades ([Tague and Grant 2009](https://doi.org/10.1029/2008WR007179)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].
- Western snowmelt timing has moved earlier by roughly 1-4 weeks, and the Pacific Northwest is among the most sensitive regions ([Stewart et al. 2005](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70029010); [Regonda et al. 2005](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_17451)) [verified: opened].
- Reverse-ESP is the accepted framework for separating initial-condition from forcing contributions to forecast skill ([Wood and Lettenmaier 2008](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2008GL034648)) [verified: opened].

**Contested or mixed.** Five questions lack consensus.

- **Average skill gain from soil moisture.** Goble and Schumacher find none on average in western Colorado but a gain in 2020-21 [verified: opened]. A USU thesis reports soil moisture was "consistently the best" incremental predictor added to CBRFC forecasts, without numbers on the page ([Morovati 2025](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/etd2023/650)) [verified: opened]. A North Platte study found it improved only the worst forecasts ([Oubeidillah et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2306-5338/6/2/50)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].
- **How much of the 2021 miss was soil versus weather.** CBRFC attributes it mainly to a record-dry April with the modelled low efficiency already captured ([CBRFC WY2021](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/yir/yir_2021.pdf)) [verified: opened]. Agency narratives and the 2021 papers emphasise dry soils. No formal attribution partition was found.
- **Initial-state versus climate skill in the Columbia.** At October 1, Columbia skill comes mainly from winter climate forecasts with a small soil-moisture contribution ([Hamlet et al. 2004 abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/84Annual/webprogram/Paper71113.html)) [verified: opened]. In the Rio Grande, a late-October initial state greatly reduces variance for up to four months ([Wood and Lettenmaier 2008](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2008GL034648)) [verified: opened]. The result is basin and season dependent.
- **Whether fast melt raises efficiency.** The mechanism that rapid melt brings soil to field capacity and raises efficiency ([Barnhart et al. 2016](https://doaj.org/article/ac6ead42cdb64b73a56cf1a3137d61a9)) is abstract-level only [lower confidence: search snippet only].
- **Whether coordinated NRCS/RFC forecasts were discontinued after 2011.** One source says so; the undated CBRFC document describes coordination as ongoing. This is unresolved in the notes.

**Unknown (the notes searched and found no study).**

- A quantified fill-and-spill threshold, such as the mm of soil deficit that must be filled before efficiency recovers.
- A peer-reviewed regression of April-July (or April-September) runoff efficiency on fall soil moisture.
- Any Columbia/PNW quantification of fall soil moisture to spring runoff efficiency. The Pacific Northwest 2021 account found is a single news item reporting a near-average northern and below-average southern forecast, with no soil-moisture quantification ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=47456)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].
- A PNW snow-on (snow onset) date trend study.
- PNW baseflow/recession studies for Columbia River Basalt, Idaho batholith and Belt metasedimentary terrain.
- Any published Columbia study of the fraction of April-July/April-September volume variance attributable to fall soil moisture.
- Attribution of 2001, 2005, 2023 or 2024 Columbia forecast error to fall soil moisture.

## 2. Dry antecedent soils cost runoff, but quantification stops at Colorado and California

### Operational statements outrun the peer-reviewed numbers

CBRFC states that basins with below-average soil moisture have lower runoff efficiency until soil-moisture deficits are filled, that above-average soil moisture makes rain or melt runoff more efficient, and that it revises model soil moisture each fall using early-November baseflow, reservoir inflows, July-October precipitation and past-season runoff ([CBRFC Feb 2024 briefing](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/2024/cowsupfeb2024.pdf); [CBRFC Apr 2022 briefing](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/2022/cowsupapr2022.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. A 2019 CBRFC discussion adds that fall soil conditions are less informative in the Upper Colorado than in northern basins that stay under snow all winter ([CBRFC 2019-04-01 discussion](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/wsup/pub2/discussion/cbrfc.2019.4.1.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. That caveat matters for us: Columbia headwaters resemble the "northern basins" more than the Upper Colorado.

The one quantitative operational figure is from CBRFC's WY2021 Year in Review: fall 2020 modelled soil moisture was among the driest of 1981-2020, and an internal analysis gave **roughly 0.5% lower annual runoff per 1.0% lower fall soil moisture** ([CBRFC, "Water Year in Review 2021," Oct 2022, NWS Colorado Basin River Forecast Center](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/yir/yir_2021.pdf)) [verified: opened]. The underlying CBRFC Model Sensitivity Analysis (October 2020) was not opened, so the definition of "soil moisture" and the basins covered are unknown. This is a model-based internal estimate, not a peer-reviewed observational regression.

The peer-reviewed anchor is Goble and Schumacher (2023, "On the Sources of Water Supply Forecast Error in Western Colorado," *J. Hydrometeorology* 24(12):2321-2332, doi:10.1175/JHM-D-23-0004.1). They found that on average antecedent root-zone soil moisture and groundwater storage add no significant seasonal skill, but they improved accuracy in 2020 and 2021, when runoff fell below what snowpack alone implied ([NOAA IR record](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/64150)) [verified: opened]. The NIDIS summary adds that most hindcast error arises from weather after 1 April, and that WLDAS soil moisture lowered April 1 hindcast error in all four major basins, with large gains in 2021 ([NIDIS/Drought.gov](https://www.drought.gov/news/understanding-water-supply-forecast-error-colorado-river-2024-01-09)) [verified: opened]. No numeric skill scores were available from either page.

*Inference (carried from the notes, not a finding):* "No skill on average, skill in dry years" is consistent with a nonlinear or threshold effect, but it does not demonstrate one. Regional contrasts in sensitivity may reflect storage capacity (Brooks et al. 2025); no paper found maps soil-moisture sensitivity by region.

### Water year 2021 is the natural experiment

The Western Water Assessment summary of WY2021 reports April 1 SWE at 50-80% of normal at most locations but April-July streamflow of only **8-57% of normal** at the same locations, Lake Powell inflow at 28% of normal, and soils in large areas of Utah and western Colorado starting the water year at their driest since 1948 ([WWA WY2021 summary](https://wwa.colorado.edu/resources/intermountain-west-climate-dashboard/briefing/water-year-2021-summary)) [verified: opened]. McEvoy and Hatchett (2023, *Environmental Research Letters* 18, 014007; title not recorded in the notes) found record 7-day melt rates at 24% of SNOTEL sites, soils critically dry entering winter 2020-21 after a dry, warm autumn, and antecedent dryness reducing runoff efficiency. Snow drought was widespread (41% of stations between the 5th and 20th percentile) but rarely extreme (9% below the 5th). The paper gives no numeric runoff-efficiency values ([DOI 10.1088/1748-9326/aca8bd](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/aca8bd)) [verified: opened].

California shows the same pattern in agency material only. CNRFC expected poor efficiency because soil below snow was depleted, and the April 1 outlook was roughly 35-55% of normal April-July runoff despite snow-course readings of 60-80% ([CNRFC WRUpdate 2021-03-11](https://www.cnrfc.noaa.gov/WaterResourcesUpdates/WRUpdate20210311.pdf); [CNRFC WY2021 Recap](https://www.cnrfc.noaa.gov/WY_summaries/WY_2021_Recap.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. The Water Year 2025 Upper Colorado repeat (Lake Powell inflow 41% of average despite April 1 SWE at 87%, attributed partly to early melt and extremely dry soils) suggests the failure mode recurs ([WWA WY2025 summary](https://wwa.colorado.edu/resources/intermountain-west-dashboard/briefing/water-year-2025-summary)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

For the Pacific Northwest the notes found only an EIA item reporting that the NWRFC April 1 2021 forecast was near-average in the northern half of the Columbia basin and below average in the southern half after a hot, dry summer 2020, with no soil-moisture quantification ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=47456)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. *Inference (notes):* because April weather, melt rate and ET also changed in 2021, percent-of-normal comparisons mix soil effects with other drivers and cannot be assigned wholly to soils.

### Melt rate and snow-on timing decide where meltwater goes

Barnhart et al. (2016, "Snowmelt rate dictates streamflow," *Geophys. Res. Lett.* 43(15):8006-8016, doi:10.1002/2016GL069690) report Budyko streamflow anomalies correlated with simulated baseflow efficiency (r2 = 0.64) and with snowmelt rate (r2 = 0.42), with the proposed mechanism that rapid melt brings soil to field capacity and drives percolation below the root zone ([DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/ac6ead42cdb64b73a56cf1a3137d61a9)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. A CZO page lists a Barnhart, Molotch and Tague 2016 abstract titled "Bidirectional response of runoff to changes in snowmelt" with the same mechanism; its journal and DOI are unconfirmed and the notes do not establish whether it is the same paper ([CZO page](https://czo-archive.criticalzone.org/sierra/publications/pub/barnhart-et-al-2016-bidirectional-response-of-runoff-to-changes-in-snowmelt/)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. A HYDRUS-1D study (Hammond, Harpold, Weiss and Kampf 2019, *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 23:3553; title not recorded) reports that event runoff is higher for snowmelt than rainfall owing to higher antecedent moisture and input rates ([HESS PDF](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/23/3553/2019/hess-23-3553-2019.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. *Inference (notes):* fall dryness and slow, early melt compound, since dry soil plus slow melt keeps water in the root zone where ET removes it.

On frozen ground and snow-on timing the notes found almost nothing usable. General principles appear in a thesis on Alpine soil frost (early snowfall insulates; snow-poor winters allow deep frost and basal ice; [Bayard 2003, EPFL](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/33185?ln=en)) and in a global reanalysis showing unfrozen bottom soil under Northern Hemisphere snow about 30% of the time ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10354943)), both [lower confidence: search snippet only]. A PNW mixed-conifer item (an unreviewed CZO presentation) reports peak soil-moisture response nearly coincident with snow disappearance ([USFS TreeSearch](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/25559)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. **No primary source was found on snow-on date relative to soil wetting, on basal-melt contribution to soil moisture, or on concrete versus granular frost in mountain basins.** *Inference (notes, unsupported by a source found):* in maritime PNW basins, soil under snow is typically near 0 C and unfrozen, so frozen-ground suppression should matter less than in continental basins and dry-fall effects should run mainly through storage deficits.

### Snow drought type, warming and groundwater modulate efficiency

In 172 minimally disturbed watersheds, every snow-drought type in every region had lower annual streamflow, maximum and minimum flows and runoff ratios, plus more low-flow days and earlier timing, and consecutive droughts reduced runoff ratios further, which the authors attribute likely to depleted subsurface storage ([Hammond, Kampf et al. 2024, *Hydrological Sciences Journal*, doi:10.1080/02626667.2024.2390919; full title not recorded](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70258230)) [verified: opened]. Brooks et al. (2025, *Communications Earth & Environment*, doi:10.1038/s43247-025-02303-3; title as it appears in the USGS URL slug, "Groundwater dominates snowmelt runoff and controls streamflow efficiency in the western [US]") report that groundwater supplied 58% +/- 34% of snowmelt runoff, mean streamwater age was 5.7 +/- 4.3 years, and low-permeability hard-rock or shale catchments had younger water, less storage and more efficient streamflow generation than high-permeability sandstone catchments ([USGS record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/groundwater-dominates-snowmelt-runoff-and-controls-streamflow-efficiency-western)) [verified: opened]. The authors conclude that runoff reflects several prior years of climate filtered through groundwater storage.

Warming-driven efficiency loss is documented for the Colorado River. Udall and Overpeck (2017, WRR; title per URL slug "The twenty-first century Colorado River hot drought and implications for the future") attribute about one-third (range one-sixth to one-half) of the 19% 2000-2014 flow loss to temperature ([Univ. of Arizona](https://swcasc.arizona.edu/publication/twenty-first-century-colorado-river-hot-drought-and-implications-future)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Woodhouse and Pederson (2018, *Water Resour. Res.* 54:286-300, doi:10.1002/2017WR021663; title not recorded) reconstruct ~400 years of Upper Colorado runoff efficiency and find temperature probably dominant ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70195228)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Milly and Dunne (2020, *Science*) report about 9% flow loss per degree C, but only press coverage was seen, so **the figures are unconfirmed** ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/climate-change-warming-slowly-drying-up-colorado-river-water)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Berghuijs, Woods and Hrachowitz (2014, "A precipitation shift from snow towards rain leads to a decrease in streamflow," *Nature Climate Change* 4:583-586, doi:10.1038/nclimate2246) estimate ~10% annual streamflow decrease for 2 C warming on average, with wide variation; the catchment count differs between sources (420 vs 97) ([VU repository](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/a-precipitation-shift-from-snow-towards-rain-leads-to-a-decrease--3/)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Livneh and Badger (2020, "Drought less predictable under declining future snowpack," *Nature Climate Change* 10:452-458) find snow's skill at predicting seasonal drought declines in 69% of snowmelt-dominated western areas by mid-century and 83% by late century, via snowpack approaching zero or lower warm-season runoff efficiency ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcli/v10y2020i5d10.1038_s41558-020-0754-8.html)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

## 3. Forecasters carry soil moisture as hidden model state, not as a predictor

### Statistical forecasts use SWE and precipitation, sometimes prior flow

The notes found no evidence that operational NRCS regression equations use soil moisture. NRCS daily-updating forecasts combine SNOTEL SWE and water-year-to-date precipitation using Z-score regression, with skill highest in Idaho and southern Colorado (Pagano, Garen, Perkins and Pasteris 2009, "Daily Updating of Operational Statistical Seasonal Water Supply Forecasts for the Western U.S.," *J. American Water Resources Assoc.* 45(3):767-778; [NRCS PDF, fetch timed out](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2023-03/DAILY%20UPDATING%20OF%20OPERATIONAL%20STATISTICAL%20SEASONAL%20WATER%20SUPPLY%20FORECASTS%20FOR%20THE%20WESTERN%20U.S..pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. NRCS-style predictors include precipitation, previous-season streamflow volume (a persistence or baseflow proxy) and SWE ([Oubeidillah, Tootle and Piechota 2019, "Incorporating Antecedent Soil Moisture into Streamflow Forecasting," *Hydrology* 6(2):50, doi:10.3390/hydrology6020050](https://www.mdpi.com/2306-5338/6/2/50)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. In the North Platte test, adding antecedent soil moisture improved the poorest forecasts but left overall skill unchanged [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Montana DNRC documentation lists SWE, precipitation, antecedent flow and a climate index, and uses principal components because nearby SNOTEL and precipitation stations are collinear ([Upper Teton documentation](https://www.dnrc.mt.gov/_docs/water/Hydro_science_data/Upper_Teton_Water_Supply_Forecast_Documentation.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

The CBRFC "Water Supply Forecasting Tools" document (undated, [CBRFC](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/wsup/doc/cbrfc_ws_tools.pdf)) [verified: opened] describes statistical water supply (SWS) regressions on monthly total precipitation, first-of-month SWE at several sites, monthly flow volume and climate indices, with output as a conditional 10/50/90% exceedance distribution. Its worked Dillon Reservoir April 1 example uses three SNOTEL SWE values and November-March precipitation at two gauges, one principal component, R2 = 0.60, n = 30 (1971-2000), and no flow or soil-moisture term. CBRFC's preferred forecast blends SWS, ESP and forecaster judgment; official forecasts issue on the first of each month January-May [verified: opened]. Garen (1992, "Improved Techniques in Regression-Based Streamflow Volume Forecasting," *J. Water Resources Planning and Management* 118(6):654-670, doi:10.1061/(ASCE)0733-9496(1992)118:6(654); author attribution inferred from citing literature) lists using only data available at forecast time, principal components regression, cross-validation and systematic variable search ([ASCE](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9496%281992%29118%3A6%28654%29)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

*Inference (notes):* the antecedent term in NRCS/CBRFC equations is mostly observed prior streamflow or accumulated precipitation, both indirect proxies for fall/winter storage. Prior flow is a natural slot to test fall baseflow as a covariate. The notes could not open NRCS National Water and Climate Center methodology documents, so point-specific Columbia predictor lists are unknown.

### Snow-17/SAC-SMA and ESP pass soil moisture forward only as state

Both CBRFC and NWRFC run Snow-17 and SAC-SMA continuously. CBRFC's model computes runoff "using a soil moisture model"; SWE is not a direct input, and snow states are nudged each winter month against SNOTEL. ESP starts from operational model states, optionally uses short-range QPF/QTF, and resamples historical forcings; ESP "keeps track of soil moisture states" but SWS does not ([CBRFC tools document](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/wsup/doc/cbrfc_ws_tools.pdf)) [verified: opened]. CBRFC's WY2021 review states that about **half of April 1 volume error comes from unknown spring weather and half from model error**, the latter including soil-moisture states, snow/SWE, parameters and structure ([CBRFC WY2021](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/yir/yir_2021.pdf)) [verified: opened]. NWRFC briefings describe ESP driven by model states and climatological forcings (WY1949-2018 in those found) with 0/5/10-day QPF/QTF variants, distinguish a "Water Supply ESP" built for the Columbia River Treaty from "ESP Natural," and note that exceedance probabilities capture only climatological-forcing uncertainty ([NWRFC 2019-06-06](https://www.nwrfc.noaa.gov/presentations/html/wy2019_ws/WS_Briefing_Page_20190606.pdf); [NWRFC 2020-01-09](https://www.nwrfc.noaa.gov/presentations/html/wy2020_ws/WS_Briefing_Page_20200109.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. *Inference (notes):* if SAC-SMA stores are mis-estimated without direct soil-moisture assimilation, the forecast inherits the error. The notes found no public NWRFC technical note on initial-state updating, bias correction or post-processing, and no Columbia-specific forecast-versus-observed series at The Dalles.

CBRFC frames its WY2021 forecasts as having captured the low efficiency: observed April volumes ranked in the driest 10th percentile, below the January 90% exceedance forecast, which CBRFC said was to be expected given the record-dry April ([CBRFC WY2021](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/yir/yir_2021.pdf)) [verified: opened]. Claims that the forecast "missed" because of dry soil rest on the Goble and Schumacher research, whose finding is that soil-moisture information helped hindcasts but post-1 April weather was the largest error source.

### Does soil moisture improve skill? Average little, dry years a lot

Mendoza et al. (2017, "An intercomparison of approaches for improving operational seasonal streamflow forecasts," *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 21:3915-3935, doi:10.5194/hess-21-3915-2017) benchmarked regression and ESP in five PNW basins: ESP had high correlation skill but larger bias, climate predictors helped most where teleconnections are strong and predictability low, and the hierarchical HESP approach was reliable at all initialization times ([UCAR record](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/an-intercomparison-of-approaches-for-improving-operational-season/)) [verified: opened]. Morovati (2025, Utah State University MS thesis, "Evaluating Use of Multiple Hydrologic Storage Indicators to Enhance Streamflow Forecasting") tested SWE, January baseflow as a groundwater proxy and soil moisture in the Upper Colorado and Great Salt Lake basins; soil moisture was "consistently the best at incrementally improving" NWS forecasts, with combined indicators best in undisturbed headwaters. The page gives no numbers and a thesis is not peer reviewed ([USU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/etd2023/650)) [verified: opened].

The Bureau of Reclamation Water Supply Forecast Rodeo (26 western sites, 0.10/0.50/0.90 quantile volumes) produced winners who used site statics, day of year, local precipitation and temperature, USGS streamflow and SWE, plus NRCS/RFC naturalised antecedent flow, SWANN, gridMET PDSI (3 winning models), ERA5-Land (2) and SEAS5 (2); soil moisture as such was not named, and teleconnection indices ended up in no final model. Cross-validation scores clustered around 85-90 KAF, best 79.5 KAF ([DrivenData final winners](https://blog.drivendata.org/blog/water-supply-forecast-and-final-winners); [hindcast winners](https://blog.drivendata.org/blog/water-supply-hindcast-winners)) [verified: opened]. Neither post compared winners with operational forecasts, and individual model reports were not opened. *Inference (notes):* antecedent streamflow and PDSI are plausible indirect storage proxies in those models.

Dettinger (2007, AGU Fall Meeting abstract, not a peer-reviewed article; title not recorded) states that SWE is the most valuable predictor for much of the cool season, that before snowfall the main (weak) source is ENSO, and that under uniform +2 C warming March forecasts lose about 15% of captured variance and antecedent measures become relatively more important ([USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70157343)) [verified: opened].

**Unreliable figure.** Ghimire, Vivoni and Wang (2026, "Fall soil moisture modulates snow-streamflow dynamics in the Colorado River Basin," *Water Resources Research*) ran VIC experiments holding snowpack constant while varying 1 October soil moisture and April-June climate. One ASU page reports that fall soil moisture explains 69-77% of Upper Basin streamflow variability and another about 74% versus about 24% for spring precipitation. An ASU list still shows the paper "In Review," titles differ across sources, and the DOI could not be confirmed ([ASU CHI page](https://chi.asu.edu/?p=1607)) [verified: opened for the page; the figures are not reliable and are press/web-summary level]. These are model-experiment variance attributions, not forecast skill, and should not be cited as a published result until the paper is confirmed.

### Initial-condition experiments: the closest precedent to our design

Wood and Lettenmaier (2008, "An ensemble approach for attribution of hydrologic prediction uncertainty," *Geophys. Res. Lett.* 35, L14401, doi:10.1029/2008GL034648) compare ESP (perfect initial conditions, resampled forcing) with reverse-ESP (resampled initial conditions, perfect forcing). At Shasta (northern California), late-October ESP error is near climatology because stores are depleted, so skill must come from future climate. At Lobatos on the Rio Grande, a late-October start with known initial conditions greatly reduces variance for up to four months, because late-summer precipitation drives soil moisture that carries into fall/winter baseflow; roles swap at 5-6 months, and initial conditions matter again by late April ([GRL PDF](https://www.wcrp-climate.org/images/summer_school/ICTP_2014/documents/wood_lettenmeier_2008_georesletter.pdf)) [verified: opened]. Hamlet, Wood, Babu and Lettenmaier (2004, "Sources of skill and error in long range Columbia River streamflow forecasts: a comparison of the role of hydrologic state variables and winter climate forecasts," AMS 84th Annual Meeting, conference abstract) found October 1 Columbia skill primarily from winter climate forecasts with a small initial-soil-moisture contribution, and by December 1 more dependence on hydrologic-state persistence ([AMS abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/84Annual/webprogram/Paper71113.html)) [verified: opened]. Unconditional NCEP climate-model hindcasts added practically no skill over ESP (Wood, Kumar and Lettenmaier 2005, *J. Geophys. Res.*, doi:10.1029/2004JD004508; [CIG UW](https://cig.uw.edu/publications/a-retrospective-assessment-of-national-centers-for-environmental-prediction-climate-model-based-ensemble-hydrologic-forecasting-in-the-western-united-states)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Digest-level statements attributed to Li et al. (2009) and to a Lettenmaier AGU 2015 abstract (initial-condition errors dominate to about a month) could not be verified from primary text and are not relied on.

### Columbia post-mortems point to temperature, not soil

For 2015 the April 9 official April-September forecast at The Dalles was 82% of normal; observed runoff was 77% of normal for April 1-May 6 but 113% for October 1-May 6, and NWRFC attributed the shortfall to rain rather than snow and early melt ([NWRFC 2015-04-09](https://www.nwrfc.noaa.gov/presentations/html/wy2015_ws/WS_Briefing_Page_20150409.pdf); [2015-05-07](https://www.nwrfc.noaa.gov/presentations/html/wy2015_ws/WS_Briefing_Page_20150507.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. In 2024, NWRFC said forecasts correlated largely with peak snowpack, with well-below-normal forecasts in the north and well-above in the south ([NWRFC 2024-05-02](https://www.nwrfc.noaa.gov/presentations/html/wy2024_ws/WS_Briefing_Page_20240502.pdf); [NW Council](https://www.nwcouncil.org/news/2024/05/21/water-supply-forecast/)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. None of the sources mentions soil moisture. Whether fall dryness contributed to 2001, 2005, 2023 or 2024 error is undocumented in the notes.

## 4. Geology sets how long storage remembers: baseflow, recession and the multi-year question

### Oregon Cascades give the clearest geologic framework, and nothing else in the PNW is quantified

Tague and Grant (2004, *Water Resour. Res.*, doi:10.1029/2003WR002629; exact title not recorded, the notes describe it as a geologic framework for interpreting low-flow regimes of Cascade streams in the Willamette basin) established the framework ([Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1029/2003wr002629)) [verified: opened]. Tague and Grant (2009, "Groundwater dynamics mediate low-flow response to global warming in snow-dominated alpine regions," *Water Resour. Res.* 45, W07421, doi:10.1029/2008WR007179) built a conceptual model linking snowmelt-peak timing and geologically mediated recession: slower-draining watersheds hold more summer water but lose the most under 1.5 C warming, with a slow-draining High Cascades basin losing about **four times** the summer flow of faster-draining basins at similar melt timing, and high-elevation granitic Sierra basins also highly sensitive ([OSU WPG page](https://wpg.forestry.oregonstate.edu/groundwater-dynamics-mediate-low-flow-response-global-warming-snow-dominated-alpine-regions)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Safeeq, Grant, Lewis and Tague (2013, "Coupling snowpack and groundwater dynamics to interpret historical streamflow trends in the western United States," *Hydrological Processes* 27:655-668, doi:10.1002/hyp.9628) used 81 watersheds (1950-2010) with recession constant k and snow fraction Sf: summer flow is most sensitive in slow-draining snow-receiving watersheds, spring flow in fast-draining ones, and late-fall/winter declines occur even in rain-dominated basins ([OSU PDF](https://archive-wpg.forestry.oregonstate.edu/sites/wpg/files/bibliopdfs/13_Safeeqetal_HP.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Jefferson (2011, *Geophys. Res. Lett.* 38, L16402; single-author, title not recorded) found across 29 PNW watersheds that basins with more than 50% of area in the seasonal snow zone showed significant trends toward greater winter and lower summer discharge ([OSU WPG](https://wpg.forestry.oregonstate.edu/node/740)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Tague et al. (2008, "Deep groundwater mediates streamflow response to climate warming in the Oregon Cascades," *Climatic Change*, doi:10.1007/s10584-007-9294-8; Crossref lists 2007) belong with this cluster; the title is verified by Crossref, while the High-Cascades summer-flow result is from a search summary ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-007-9294-8)) [verified: opened for the record; lower confidence: search snippet only for the finding].

Deschutes evidence is USGS hydrogeology: Gannett et al. (2001, USGS WRIR 00-4162) put average annual recharge near 3,500 ft3/s with the Cascades as the main recharge zone and roughly half of groundwater discharging to spring-fed streams at the range margins ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri20004162)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. For Columbia River Basalt, the USGS Columbia Plateau study describes about 44,000 mi2 with aquifers in interflow zones separated by low-permeability flow interiors, compartmentalised by faulting, and with declining groundwater levels reducing streamflow and baseflow ([USGS landing page](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/washington-water-science-center/science/columbia-plateau-groundwater-availability-study)) [verified: opened]. For the Idaho batholith the notes have only a low-dissolved-solids note (about 50 mg/L in granitic Boise River streams) and a GSA abstract calling batholith recharge to Boise valley aquifers important but unquantified ([USGS WRI 82-4006](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri824006); [GSA 2005](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2005AM/webprogram/Paper96582.html)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. **There is no peer-reviewed headwater recession or baseflow-index study found for CRBG-dominated, Idaho batholith, Belt metasediment or glaciated PNW basins.** Karst is the one analogue found outside volcanics: Tennant et al. (2024, "Karst Hydrologic Memory Supplements Streamflow During Dry Periods in Snow-Dominated, Mountainous Watersheds," *Hydrological Processes*) report that low flows in watersheds with more than 30% carbonate area depend strongly on the preceding winter's precipitation and streamflow ([Boise State](https://experts.boisestate.edu/en/publications/karst-hydrologic-memory-supplements-streamflow-during-dry-periods/)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

*Inference (notes):* High Cascades basins are slow, high-storage systems and Western Cascades basins flashier; high-storage basins respond less to antecedent seasonal conditions in the near term but carry multi-year memory, while low-storage granitic or thin-regolith basins have little memory but face the largest late-summer deficits. The granitic claim rests on the Sierra example in Tague and Grant (2009), not on Idaho-batholith data.

### Memory is demonstrated for one year, rarely for more

In eight Sierra catchments, a 10% drop in peak SWE corresponded to a 9-22% drop in annual minimum flow with minima 3-7 days earlier, and low flows at Sagehen and Pitman Creeks tracked previous-year snowpack as well (Godsey, Kirchner and Tague 2014, "Effects of changes in winter snowpacks on summer low flows: case studies in the Sierra Nevada, California, USA," *Hydrological Processes* 28:5048-5064, doi:10.1002/hyp.9943; title per the secondary-summary URL slug; figures from a secondary summary) ([Climate Signals summary](https://www.climatesignals.org/scientific-reports/effects-changes-winter-snowpacks-summer-low-flows-case-studies-sierra-nevada); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.9943)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Search summaries of Tague-group Andrews Forest work add that groundwater shapes the annual hydrograph while interannual variability is mostly climatic, and that warmer winters and earlier melt lengthened summer recession and lowered autumn minimum discharge in a groundwater-dominated basin ([Andrews Forest pub 4749](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/pubs/pdf/pub4749.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. In a low-snowpack drought year, baseflow sources in an Oregon Cascades stream network were more dependent on terrain proxies for deep-seated earthflow and Quaternary-deposit storage (Segura et al. 2019, *Water Resour. Res.*; title not recorded; study site to be verified) ([Segura PDF](https://people-facstaff.forestry.oregonstate.edu/catalina-segura/ewExternalFiles/2019_Segura_et_al-2019-Water_Resources_Research.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. **No PNW paper was found quantifying lag-2-or-longer year autocorrelation of low flow or drought persistence.**

### Dynamic storage, indirect storage and rock moisture complicate "soil moisture"

Kirchner (2009, *Water Resour. Res.* 45, W02429, doi:10.1029/2008WR006912; title not recorded in the notes) treats discharge as a function of storage with parameters derived from recession fluctuations ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1029/2008WR006912)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Staudinger et al. (2017, "Catchment water storage variation with elevation," *Hydrological Processes* 31:2000-2015, doi:10.1002/hyp.11158) and a related EGU abstract found dynamic storage from water balance, recession analysis and two bucket models differing by up to an order of magnitude ([CSDMS record](https://csdms.colorado.edu/wiki/Reference:Reference-009427); [EGU2018-4387](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2018/EGU2018-4387.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Dralle et al. (2018, "Quantification of the seasonal hillslope water storage that does not drive streamflow," *Hydrological Processes*) found that in Northern California Coast Ranges most dynamic storage is "indirect," changing without driving discharge ([PDF](https://ce.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/assets/users/thompson/Dralle_et_al-2018-Hydrological_Processes.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Rempe and Dietrich (2018, *PNAS* 115:2664-2669, doi:10.1073/pnas.1800141115; title not recorded in the notes) report up to 27% of annual rainfall seasonally stored as rock moisture at one Mendocino County hillslope ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1800141115); [PMC5856562](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856562)) [verified: opened]. Hahm et al. (2019, GRL, "Low subsurface water storage capacity relative to annual rainfall decouples Mediterranean plant productivity and water use from rainfall variability") show small storage capacity relative to winter rainfall equalises summer water availability across years in seven Coast Range catchments (DOI not confirmed; [Eel CZO archive](https://czo-archive.criticalzone.org/eel/publications/pub/hahm-et-al-2019-low-subsurface-water-storage-capacity-relative-to-annual-ra/)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Wang-Erlandsson et al. (2016, "Global root zone storage capacity from satellite-based evaporation," *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 20:1459-1481, doi:10.5194/hess-20-1459-2016) estimate root-zone storage sized to survive dry spells, with forests buffering long-return-period droughts ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-20-1459-2016)) [verified: opened].

*Inference (notes):* products measuring soil moisture at 0-1 m miss bedrock water, so the late-summer deficit that snowmelt must refill may be larger than surface soil moisture suggests. All plant-accessible bedrock-water evidence found is Californian, Mediterranean and rain-dominated; transfer to snow-dominated PNW basins is untested. Dynamic storage from recession likely underestimates total storage since indirect storage never drives discharge.

### Recession and baseflow-separation methods are method-dependent

Stoelzle, Stahl and Weiler (2013, "Are streamflow recession characteristics really characteristic?," *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 17:817-828, doi:10.5194/hess-17-817-2013) show across 20 catchments that recession methods correlate but do not consistently rank catchments, with between-method differences sometimes exceeding between-catchment differences, and recommend a multiple-methods approach ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-17-817-2013)) [verified: opened]. Dralle, Karst and Thompson (2015, "a, b careful: the challenge of scale invariance for comparative analyses in power law models of the streamflow recession," *Geophys. Res. Lett.*, doi:10.1002/2015GL066007) and Dralle, Karst, Charalampous, Veenstra and Thompson (2017, "Event-scale power law recession analysis: quantifying methodological uncertainty," *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 21:65-79, doi:10.5194/hess-21-65-2017) document scale non-invariance and extraction uncertainty ([DOI 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/2015gl066007); [DOI 2017](https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-21-65-2017)) [verified: opened]. Rupp and Selker (2006, "On the use of the Boussinesq equation for interpreting recession hydrographs from sloping aquifers," *Water Resour. Res.* 42, doi:10.1029/2006WR005080) is the Boussinesq reference ([OSU record](https://bee.oregonstate.edu/publication/use-boussinesq-equation-interpreting-recession-hydrographs-sloping-aquifers)) [verified: opened]. Biswal and Marani (2010, "Geomorphological origin of recession curves," *Geophys. Res. Lett.* 37, doi:10.1029/2010GL045415) tie the recession exponent to network geometry across 67 US basins ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1029/2010GL045415)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

For baseflow separation, Eckhardt ("How to construct recursive digital filters for baseflow separation," *Hydrological Processes*, 2004 online/2005 volume, doi:10.1002/hyp.5675) is a parameter-dependent filter ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.5675)) [verified: opened]. Gonzales, Nonner, Heijkers and Uhlenbrook (2009, *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 13:2055-2068; lowland Netherlands, title not recorded) found tracer-based approaches implying about 90% of discharge was groundwater displaced by event water and that two-component separation fails with more than two end members ([HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/13/2055/2009/)) [verified: opened]. *Inference (notes):* in snowmelt basins a filter-derived baseflow index mixes delayed melt with groundwater, so treat it as an index of delayed flow unless tracer-validated, and record filter parameters with every value. The snowmelt seasonal recession constant (Safeeq 2013 k) and event-scale recession are different quantities and should not be conflated.

## 5. Snow timing, regimes and the rain-snow transition reshape which basins are exposed

### Timing has moved earlier by weeks, with the Pacific Northwest among the most sensitive

Stewart, Cayan and Dettinger (2005, "Changes toward earlier streamflow timing across western North America," *J. Climate* 18(8):1136-1155, doi:10.1175/JCLI3321.1) analysed 302 gauges for 1948-2002 and found regionally coherent trends toward earlier snowmelt flow, with increasing fractions of annual flow arriving 1-4 weeks earlier, caused most directly by winter/spring temperature ([USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70029010)) [verified: opened]. Cayan et al. (2001, "Changes in the onset of spring in the western United States," *Bull. Amer. Meteor. Soc.* 82(3):399-415, doi:10.1175/1520-0477(2001)082<0399:CITOOS>2.3.CO;2) found earlier springs since the late 1970s, with temperature the main factor ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70023493)) [verified: opened]. Regonda, Rajagopalan, Clark and Pitlick (2005, "Seasonal cycle shifts in hydroclimatology over the western United States," *J. Climate* 18:372-384) found the largest changes at lower elevations and in the Pacific Northwest, where winter temperatures sit near melting ([CU Boulder record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_17451)) [verified: opened]. Hidalgo et al. (2009, "Detection and attribution of streamflow timing changes to climate change in the western United States," *J. Climate* 22(13):3838-3855, doi:10.1175/2009JCLI2470.1) attributed observed timing shifts to anthropogenic forcing, with the Columbia the only single basin showing a detectable signal when regions were analysed individually (the Columbia detail comes from a search summary) ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70036576)) [verified: opened for the attribution; lower confidence: search snippet only for the Columbia detail]. Fritze, Stewart and Pebesma (2011, "Shifts in western North American snowmelt runoff regimes for the recent warm decades," *J. Hydrometeorology* 12(5):989-1006; the notes' record lists J. Hydrometeorology, not J. Hydrology) is confirmed to exist but its abstract was not retrieved ([Univ. Münster record](https://cris.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/22227071)) [verified: opened for the citation only].

On snowpack, Hamlet, Mote, Clark and Lettenmaier (2005, "Effects of temperature and precipitation variability on snowpack trends in the western United States," *J. Climate* 18(21):4545-4561) attribute April 1 SWE declines mainly to warming ([CIG UW](https://cig.uw.edu/publications/effects-of-temperature-and-precipitation-variability-on-snowpack-trends-in-the-western-united-states)) [verified: opened]. Mote et al. (2018, "Dramatic declines in snowpack in the western US," *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science* 1:2, doi:10.1038/s41612-018-0012-1) report declines at more than 90% of long-record sites and roughly 15-30% loss since mid-century, largest in spring and in the Pacific states; the Nature page was not fetchable, so this is from secondary coverage ([Oregon State news](https://news.oregonstate.edu/news/new-study-snowpack-levels-show-dramatic-decline-western-states)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Kapnick and Hall (2012, "Causes of recent changes in western North American snowpack," *Climate Dynamics* 40:109-121, doi:10.1007/s00382-011-1089-y) found temperature effects appear March-May and are nearly absent in February ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00382-011-1089-y)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Sproles, Nolin, Rittger and Painter (2013, "Climate change impacts on maritime mountain snowpack in the Oregon Cascades," *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 17:2581-2597, doi:10.5194/hess-17-2581-2013) found +2 C moves McKenzie basin peak SWE about 12 days earlier and roughly halves snow storage ([HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/17/2581/2013/)) [verified: opened]. Press coverage of a Siler et al. paper claims natural Pacific decadal variability masked much of the Oregon decline (14% not significant); title, venue and DOI are unconfirmed, so it is relegated to Appendix C ([Cascade PBS](https://www.cascadepbs.org/environment/2019/01/why-hasnt-northwests-snowpack-declined/)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

### Transient basins are the most exposed, and geology modulates the result

Elsner et al. (2010, "Implications of 21st century climate change for the hydrology of Washington State," *Climatic Change* 102:225-260, doi:10.1007/s10584-010-9855-0) project April 1 SWE declines of 38-46% by the 2040s, near-total loss in three warm transient west-slope watersheds by the 2080s, and annual runoff up 2-3% from higher winter precipitation ([CIG UW](https://cig.uw.edu/publications/implications-of-21st-century-climate-change-for-the-hydrology-of-washington-state)) [verified: opened]. Tohver, Hamlet and Lee (2014, "Impacts of 21st century climate change on hydrologic extremes in the Pacific Northwest region of North America," *JAWRA*, doi:10.1111/jawr.12199) found the largest flood-risk increases in mixed rain-snow basins and strongest low-flow declines in energy-limited west-Cascade basins ([CIG UW](https://cig.uw.edu/publications/impacts-of-21st-century-climate-change-on-hydrologic-extremes-in-the-pacific-northwest-region-of-north-america-2)) [verified: opened]. Hamlet and Lettenmaier (2007, "Effects of 20th century warming and climate variability on flood risk in the western U.S.," *Water Resour. Res.* 43:W06427) found snow-dominated basins lower flood risk, rain-dominated basins little change and warm transient coastal basins increased flood risk ([CIG UW](https://cig.uw.edu/publications/effects-of-20th-century-warming-and-climate-variability-on-flood-risk-in-the-western-u-s)) [verified: opened]. Nolin and Daly (2006, "Mapping 'at risk' snow in the Pacific Northwest," *J. Hydrometeorology*, doi:10.1175/JHM543.1) map at-risk snow at about 9,200 km2 holding ~6.5 km3 of water under a 0 C threshold, concentrated in the Cascades and Olympics ([PRISM PDF](https://www.prism.oregonstate.edu/documents/pubs/2006jhydromet_snowMapping_nolin.pdf)) [verified: opened]. Safeeq, Shukla, Arismendi, Grant, Lewis and Nolin (2016, *Int. J. Climatology* 36:3175-3190, doi:10.1002/joc.4545; the note records the title imprecisely) found snow fraction varies mainly with winter temperature, with a cold-versus-warm-winter difference of ~31% in the PNW against 40% in the Sierra at low-to-mid elevations ([OSU PDF](https://archive-wpg.forestry.oregonstate.edu/sites/wpg/files/bibliopdfs/2016_Safeeqetal_SnowFraction_IJC.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. *Inference (notes):* sensitivity ranks transient > snow-dominant (timing) > rain-dominant, and groundwater storage modulates summer response independent of regime class.

### Snow-on date and fall precipitation are the least documented pieces

**No published trend in snow-on date for the PNW was located, no atmospheric-river-specific rain-snow partition citation, and no fall-precipitation trend citation.** The notes record only dataset definitions: snow onset is the first day a grid cell moves from snow-free to snow-covered in a water year in the UCLA Western US Snow Reanalysis (10% fractional snow cover; WY1985-2021, [CU Livneh group](https://ciresgroups.colorado.edu/livneh/data/western-us-gridded-snow-cover-and-timing-data-wy1985-2021)) and in a MODIS-based product (WY2001-2019, [Mendeley data](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/57852x9rrx)), both [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Rain-on-snow is better supported: Musselman et al. (2018, "Projected increases and shifts in rain-on-snow flood risk over western North America," *Nature Climate Change* 8:808-812) report flood risk up 20-200% in 55% of basins and possible decreases in lower coastal OR/WA/BC ([UCAR](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/projected-increases-and-shifts-in-rain-on-snow-flood-risk-over-we/)) [verified: opened]. WY2015 early-winter adjusted runoff was well above normal in some Washington basins (Skagit 151%, Dungeness 166%) ([NWRFC Sept 2015 outlook](https://www.drought.gov/sites/default/files/2020-06/PRE%20King%20Coastal%20PNW%20Drought%20Outlook%20Sep%202015%20NWRFC.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. *Inference (notes, untested):* later snow onset converts early snowfall to rain and soil recharge rather than storage, so fall rain wets soils but lowers early storage.

### Snow-drought typology and Columbia projections

Harpold, Dettinger and Rajagopal (2017, "Defining snow drought and why it matters," *Eos* 98, doi:10.1029/2017EO068775) define dry and warm snow drought ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70182761)) [verified: opened]. Mote et al. (2016, "Perspectives on the causes of exceptionally low 2015 snowpack in the western United States," *Geophys. Res. Lett.* 43(20):10980-10988, doi:10.1002/2016GL069965) attribute 2015 mainly to heat, with the SST contribution about twice the human contribution to OR/WA snow-drought risk ([OSU](https://water.oregonstate.edu/biblio/perspectives-causes-exceptionally-low-2015-snowpack-western-united-states)) [verified: opened]. Huning and AghaKouchak (2020, "Global snow drought hot spots and characteristics," *PNAS* 117:19753-19759, doi:10.1073/pnas.1915921117) find western US snow drought ~28% longer in the second half of 1980-2018 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7443952)) [verified: opened]. Chegwidden et al. (2019, "How do modeling decisions affect the spread among hydrologic climate change projections?...," *Earth's Future* 7(6):623-637, doi:10.1029/2018EF001047) found emissions/GCM dominate spread in annual volume and timing while the hydrologic model dominates low-flow spread across 160 simulations; the abstract-level summary does not name the Columbia ([UCAR](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/how-do-modeling-decisions-affect-the-spread-among-hydrologic-clim/)) [verified: opened]. Projections consistently show higher December-March flow and lower April-July flow; no numeric Columbia April-July fraction change or peak-date shift in days was retrieved. *Inference (notes):* dry snow droughts reduce total volume while warm ones redistribute it, so April-July volume alone conflates them.

**Metric pitfalls (all inference; no primary source found).** Center-of-mass timing can be moved by a wetter fall or winter-rain surplus without any change in snowmelt, window choice (October 1 vs January 1) and regulation can bias trends, "days from peak to 50% of peak" is not an established literature metric, and the Richards-Baker index (Baker, Richards, Loftus and Kramer 2004, JAWRA 40(2); volume and pages unverified) was developed for Midwestern streams and responds to rain events and diel melt cycles ([MN Water Research record](https://wrl.mnpals.net/index.php/taxonomy/term/7373)) [lower confidence: search snippet only].

## 6. Statistical and modeling tools exist in pieces for each of our planned tests

### Threshold physics is established; threshold statistics for fall soil moisture are not

Tromp-van Meerveld and McDonnell (2006, "Threshold relations in subsurface stormflow: 1. A 147-storm analysis of the Panola hillslope," *Water Resour. Res.* 42, doi:10.1029/2004WR003778) found a ~55 mm precipitation threshold for significant subsurface stormflow ([EarthRef](https://earthref.org/ERR/92961)) [lower confidence: search snippet only; DOI from a search summary]. Their companion (2006, "...2. The fill and spill hypothesis," *Water Resour. Res.* 42, doi:10.1029/2004WR003800) found connectivity only after >55 mm, with stormflow then rising more than fivefold ([ZORA](https://www.zora.uzh.ch/entities/publication/889bcfec-a93d-477f-85f7-b93f2f0c51ed/full)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. Zehe and Sivapalan (2009, "Threshold behaviour in hydrological systems as (human) geo-ecosystems: manifestations, controls, implications," *Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 13(7):1273-1297, doi:10.5194/hess-13-1273-2009) classify threshold forms and discuss predictability ([HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/13/1273/2009/)) [verified: opened]. These are storm-scale hillslope results, not seasonal-basin results. No western-US paper fitting a hinge or GAM of runoff efficiency on fall soil moisture was found, and the statistical citations for the planned tests (mgcv/Wood 2017, segmented regression/Muggeo 2003, quantile regression/Koenker and Bassett 1978, the Davies 1987 test, hierarchical GAMs/Pedersen et al. 2019) were not verified in the notes.

### Partial pooling has hydrologic precedent for short records

A hierarchical flood-frequency preprint reports that pooling across gauges mitigates short records ([preprints.org 202407.0789](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202407.0789)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]; a 17-site Huaihe model reduced at-site and regional uncertainty versus at-site GEV and index-flood approaches ([HESS preprint hess-2018-22](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/hess-2018-22/hess-2018-22.pdf)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]; Chen et al. used multilevel models with coefficients drawn from a common multivariate normal (*Hydrol. Earth Syst. Sci.* 18:1539, 2014; title not recorded; [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/18/1539/2014/)) [lower confidence: search snippet only]. No hydrology paper using brms, PyMC or bambi for multi-basin snow runoff was found. For model comparison, Vehtari, Gelman and Gabry (2017, "Practical Bayesian model evaluation using leave-one-out cross-validation and WAIC," *Statistics and Computing* 27(5):1413-1432, doi:10.1007/s11222-016-9696-4) provide PSIS-LOO ([arXiv 1507.04544](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.04544)) [verified: opened].

### Validation, multiplicity and benchmark practice

Roberts et al. ### Soil moisture: long records are modelled, observed records are short and flawed

ISMN lists the SNOTEL network at 509 stations from 1996-09-10, Stevens Hydraprobe analog and digital sensors, and depths from 0.05 to 2.03 m ([ISMN SNOTEL page](https://ismn.bafg.de/en/networks/?id=SNOTEL)) [verified: opened]. *Inference (notes):* mixed sensor types imply non-homogeneous calibration, and the 1996 start is the network maximum, so a station-based record cannot span 45 water years since 1982. The notes found no gridMET-specific or AORC validation. Reported issues: PRISM and Daymet spread a SNOTEL-origin warm bias over mountain terrain (Oyler and colleagues, trend-bias finding; [WSC node 1770](https://westernsnowconference.org/node/1770)); gauge-driven precipitation products disagree where SNOTEL is sparse at high elevation, and PRISM's Olympic rain-shadow gradient is too sharp ([UW ResearchWorks](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/bitstreams/350ce118-953c-4a84-92a0-ce992dca3eb6/download)); and a 2018 Colorado/Wyoming study found PRISM and Daymet acceptable at monthly time steps (*Remote Sensing* 10(12):2058, doi:10.3390/RS10122058; [MDPI](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/remotesensing/remotesensing-10-02058/article_deploy/remotesensing-10-02058.pdf)), all [lower confidence: search snippet only]. ## 8. Gaps in the literature tied to our hypothesis

Our hypothesis is that fall soil moisture at snow-on predicts spring runoff volume and efficiency, runoff timing, recession and baseflow in unregulated Columbia headwater basins, and that this effect can be isolated by hierarchical models, threshold/GAM tests and initial-condition-swap experiments. The notes support the direction and nothing more. The table states each gap plainly.

| Gap (what the notes found) | Why it matters here | How the project fills it |
|---|---|---|
| **No quantified fill-and-spill threshold** for seasonal runoff efficiency versus fall soil moisture. Threshold physics exists only at storm/hillslope scale (Panola, ~55 mm). | Goble and Schumacher's "skill only in dry years" hints at nonlinearity but does not demonstrate it. | Hinge/GAM tests with estimated threshold location and interval across basins and years. |
| **No peer-reviewed regression of spring runoff efficiency on fall soil moisture.** The only numbers are an internal CBRFC model estimate (~0.5% per 1%) and variance shares from an unpublished paper. | There is no observational effect size to compare against. | Elasticity or percent-change per 10 percentile of fall soil moisture with intervals, conditioned on SWE and weather. |
| **No Columbia/PNW quantification.** Evidence is Colorado, Great Basin, Rio Grande, California. The 2019 CBRFC remark says fall soil is less informative in basins snow-covered all winter, which describes many Columbia headwaters. | The sign and size of the effect in maritime and northern-continental snow regimes is unknown. | Six unregulated headwater basins spanning regimes; population and basin-level effects. |
| **No PNW snow-on-date trend study**, and no source on snow-on relative to soil wetting or basal melt. | Snow-on date is our timing anchor and a plausible confounder (rain versus snow in fall). | Derive snow-on per basin per year, report its trend and its link to fall soil state. |
| **No PNW baseflow/recession studies for basalt, batholith and Belt terrain**; Cascades volcanics are the only well-documented PNW geology. | Geology likely modulates memory and recession response (Brooks, Tague and Grant). | Recession constants and baseflow indices per basin with documented protocol; test geology-dependent slopes. |
| **No Columbia estimate of the variance share from fall soil moisture** in April-July/April-September volume; Hamlet et al. (2004) is a conference abstract on Oct 1 forecasts. | Reverse-ESP has not been run for Columbia headwater volume beyond that abstract. | Initial-condition-swap experiments with at least two models. |
| **No attribution of 2001, 2005, 2023, 2024 Columbia errors** to fall soil moisture; 2015 and 2024 are explained by temperature and peak snowpack. | Soil effects may be hidden among temperature-driven years. | Stratified analysis by antecedent-condition class and snow-drought type. |
| **Unresolved average-versus-dry-year skill.** Goble and Schumacher, Morovati and Oubeidillah disagree on average gain. | A model fit to all years may show little average gain while the dry-year effect is real. | Report skill by antecedent stratum, not only overall. |
| **Soil-moisture data validity in snow terrain.** SNOTEL sensors start 1996, ERA5-Land is wet-biased and weak under snow, SMAP L4 starts 2015, and bedrock water is unobserved. | The "fall soil moisture" predictor is itself uncertain. | Treat predictor uncertainty explicitly and triangulate across products and model states. |
| **Multi-year memory** is shown for one year (Sierra, karst) and in aggregate (Brooks, Hammond), with no PNW lag-2+ study. | Fall soil moisture may proxy multi-year storage rather than act causally. | Include prior-year flow and storage covariates and test whether fall soil moisture adds information. |

## Conclusion

The central lesson of this literature is a mismatch between mechanism and measurement. Everyone expects dry soils and depleted storage to tax spring runoff, and 2021 delivered a striking Colorado-centred demonstration, yet the two best peer-reviewed forecast analyses show the effect surfacing mainly in anomalous years, and the single operational coefficient is an internal model figure from a different climate regime. A study that estimates the effect on observed, unregulated Columbia headwater flows with stratified skill reporting would be the first of its kind in the notes, and its stratification by antecedent condition is the design feature most likely to separate it from the null average results.

Two caveats should travel with any result. First, because fall soil moisture is correlated with groundwater state, ENSO-linked precipitation persistence and prior-year runoff, even a clean statistical effect may reflect multi-year storage (Brooks et al. 2025, Hammond et al. 2024) rather than soil water itself, so the initial-condition-swap experiment and the prior-storage covariates carry as much weight as the regression. Second, the predictor is modelled rather than observed over most of the record, so conclusions inherit model structure, which is why the swap experiments should use more than one hydrologic model.
